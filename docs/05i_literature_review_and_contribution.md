# Literature review and contribution

## Research question

How do government farm-support payments affect farmer welfare and bank risk
when transmitted through imperfectly competitive agricultural banking markets?

Farmers and other eligible agricultural producers receive statutory support
payments directly; banks are not program beneficiaries. The banking channel
begins only after a payment changes borrower liquidity, deposits, operating-credit
demand, repayment risk, or government-supported credit outside commercial banks.
The empirical question is how the initial farmer benefit is propagated,
attenuated, or redistributed by the existing banking market.

## Literature

### Government support and agricultural markets

Agricultural-policy research has examined how support changes production,
commodity marketing, prices, and expectations. Hennessy (1998) shows that income
support can affect production through risk even when payments appear decoupled.
Janzen, Swearingen, and Yu (2023) find that Market Facilitation Program payments
allowed grain producers to delay sales and increase storage, shifting supply
across time. Sun's AEPP manuscript complements this literature by showing that
pre-official MFP and CFAP announcements changed corn and soybean futures
volatility even when official announcements had little additional effect.

### Distribution, political incidence, and use of payments

Giri, Subedi, and Kassel (2022) document heterogeneous MFP and CFAP payments
across producer groups, while Janzen, Malone, Schaefer, and Scheitrum (2023)
study political returns to ad hoc payments. Roberts, Kirwan, and Hopkins (2003),
Goodwin, Mishra, and Ortalo-Magne (2003), and Kirwan (2009) show why statutory
recipients need not retain the full economic benefit when support is capitalized
into land values and rents. USDA research also emphasizes that payments can be
consumed, saved, invested, or used to repay debt, particularly when farmers face
liquidity and credit constraints. A local deposit response must therefore be
estimated rather than assumed.

### Agricultural credit, repayment risk, and banks

Key (2020) finds that household income can relax farm credit constraints and
increase borrowing and investment. Walraven and Barry (2003) show that banks
charge higher rates on agricultural loans they classify as riskier. Recent work
also relates ARC, PLC, MFP, and CFAP payments to non-real-estate farm debt and
delinquency. This literature motivates borrower-liquidity and default-risk
channels, but generally does not jointly model deposits, wholesale funding,
securities, bank capital, and agricultural credit.

### Financial intermediation and market power

Wang, Whited, Wu, and Xiao (2020) develop a dynamic bank model in which deposit
market power, loan market power, capital regulation, and reserve requirements
jointly determine lending responses. This project applies that intermediation
logic to fiscal farm support. The government pays eligible producers; banks then
choose how to price deposits and loans and allocate their balance sheets under
the market power estimated in agricultural banking data.

## Contribution

- Add a financial-intermediation channel to research on commodity markets,
  political allocation, land incidence, and farmers' use of payments.
- Separate the direct beneficiary from the indirect intermediary: farmers
  receive support, whereas banks respond through deposits, credit demand,
  repayment risk, and outside credit.
- Combine county policy exposure, historical transmission estimates, BLP
  agricultural-bank market power, and a dynamic bank balance-sheet model.
- Evaluate farmer welfare and bank risk jointly, recognizing that lower borrowing
  can represent improved liquidity rather than a contraction in credit supply.
- Use OBBBA as a forward-looking case study and label its results as ex ante
  counterfactual predictions, not realized post-policy evidence.
